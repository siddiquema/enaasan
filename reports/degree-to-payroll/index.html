From Degree to Payroll

Report No. 1 counted who India's colleges send out. Report No. 2 showed that a degree alone does not guarantee a job. This report asks the question a family weighs when choosing a course and a city: after a degree, how likely is a young person to get onto a formal payroll, in which state, and what does salaried work pay there? It compares West Bengal, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Kerala, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and West Bengal's eastern neighbours, using only official data.

Where a young person studies and where they look for work both shape the odds of a formal, salaried start. Four states in this comparison — Maharashtra, Karnataka, Telangana and Tamil Nadu — each added between 9.7 and 11.1 young people to the EPFO payroll for every 100 people aged 18–23 in 2023-24. In West Bengal, Andhra Pradesh, Kerala and the eastern states the figure was 2.8 or lower. The household survey (PLFS 2025) points the same way for most states: more of their workers hold regular salaried jobs, and fewer of those jobs lack social security.

Three things complicate the simple picture. Kerala has few EPFO entrants but the highest share of regular salaried work in the set (40.0%), so a low EPFO figure is not the whole story. Graduate unemployment is high in several states that produce many graduates: 21.6% in Andhra Pradesh and 17.6% in Telangana. And in every state compared, women graduates face higher unemployment than men. Average pay for regular employees runs from ₹27,169 a month in Maharashtra to ₹16,618 in West Bengal.

None of this measures a placement rate, and none of it says why states differ. It tells a family where formal jobs have been showing up, so that a course choice can be made with open eyes.

Findings

Finding 1 Data

Some states produce far more graduates for their size than others

Tamil Nadu's colleges reported 6,19,629 undergraduate pass-outs in 2023-24, or 9.1 for every 100 people aged 18–23 living in the state. Karnataka (6.8), Andhra Pradesh (6.5) and Telangana (6.5) follow. West Bengal (3.5), Bihar (3.5), Jharkhand (3.3) and Assam (2.6) send out far fewer graduates relative to their young population.

This is supply: how many people finish a degree each year. On its own it says nothing about jobs. It matters because a state with many graduates and few formal openings will see more competition for each opening, at home or elsewhere.

Finding 2 Data

Formal-payroll entry is concentrated in four states

In 2023-24, EPFO's net payroll for people aged 18–25 grew by 14,17,724 in Maharashtra, 7,11,135 in Karnataka, 6,61,520 in Tamil Nadu and 3,94,357 in Telangana. Per 100 people aged 18–23, that is 11.1, 10.8, 9.7 and 10.3. Every other state in the comparison was at 2.8 or below: Kerala 2.8, Andhra Pradesh 2.3, West Bengal 2.1, Odisha 1.9, Jharkhand 1.0, Assam 0.7 and Bihar 0.3.

The gap is not an artefact of the age band. Counting only the 22–25 group, closer to graduate age, the four states range from 4.5 to 5.9 per 100 and the others from 0.2 to 1.8.

What this ratio is — and is not"Formal entry per 100 young people" is EPFO net payroll for ages 18–25 divided by the AISHE population aged 18–23. It is a ratio, not a placement rate: the two age bands differ, and the EPFO count is not limited to graduates. EPFO counts only payroll covered by provident fund — not government jobs outside EPF, not most small firms, not self-employment. Net payroll is new subscribers, minus exits, plus members who rejoined; it includes people switching employers or newly brought under coverage. EPFO does not say how it assigns a worker to a state; it may be by the employer's registration, which would credit head-office states. That attribution is unconfirmed.

Finding 3 Data

The household survey mostly agrees — Kerala is the exception

EPFO records where payroll is registered. PLFS asks households what work their members do, wherever they live. In the four high-entry states, 27.1% to 37.3% of all workers hold a regular wage or salaried job. In West Bengal it is 22.3%, Andhra Pradesh 23.6%, and in Odisha, Assam, Jharkhand and Bihar between 12.0% and 17.9%.

Kerala breaks the pattern. It sits near West Bengal on EPFO entry (2.8) but has the highest regular-job share in the set, 40.0%. The data here cannot say why; a family in Kerala should not read the EPFO figure alone.

Quality differs too. Among regular non-farm employees, 60.5% in West Bengal and 61.3% in Bihar have no social security benefit, against 36.4% in Karnataka and 43.0% in Telangana.

Finding 4 Data

Graduate unemployment varies widely, and is higher for women everywhere

Among graduates aged 15 and above who are working or looking for work, 21.6% are unemployed in Andhra Pradesh, 18.1% in Bihar, 17.6% in Telangana and 16.0% in Odisha. The rate is 11.4% in West Bengal and 11.2% in Tamil Nadu, matching India's 11.2%. Karnataka (7.9%) and Maharashtra (6.8%) are lowest among the large states.

In every state compared, women graduates face higher unemployment than men: 36.4% against 15.2% in Bihar, 30.3% against 18.5% in Andhra Pradesh, 21.5% against 7.7% in West Bengal. Fewer women graduates are in the labour force at all — 40.2% in West Bengal, 24.5% in Bihar, 53.7% in Kerala.

Telangana shows that high formal entry and high graduate unemployment can sit together: many graduates, many formal jobs, and still many graduates searching.

Finding 5 Data

Regular jobs pay most in Maharashtra, Telangana and Karnataka, and in their big cities

Across all regular wage and salaried employees — not only graduates — average monthly earnings in 2025 were ₹27,169 in Maharashtra, ₹27,052 in Telangana and ₹26,266 in Karnataka, against ₹22,699 for India. Tamil Nadu, the fourth high-entry state, averages ₹23,798, below Kerala's ₹25,443. West Bengal's average, ₹16,618, is the 2nd lowest of 35 States/UTs. For women the West Bengal average is ₹11,528.

City figures show the same spread more sharply: ₹36,267 in Greater Mumbai, ₹34,323 in Bengaluru and ₹31,153 in Hyderabad, against ₹19,093 in Kolkata. Kolkata's women regular employees average ₹13,784.

Close-up Data

West Bengal: what the "458,000" figure is

A figure of about 458,000 formal jobs for West Bengal has been circulating. It comes from EPFO: West Bengal's net payroll for FY2024-25, all ages, was 4,57,697. It is a net payroll number, not a count of jobs created. It includes people moving between employers, people newly brought under EPF coverage and people rejoining, and it covers every age.

  • 4,88,262 (FY2022-23), 4,76,439 (FY2023-24) and 4,57,697 (FY2024-25): West Bengal net payroll, all ages.
  • 2,12,064 of the FY2024-25 total were aged 18–25, and 1,12,691 were aged 22–25, the band closest to new graduates.

Practitioner view Opinion — not data

[Siddique Ahamed to write: commentary from hiring experience, in the first person. No numbers unless they come from a cited source above.]

Where students and parents can start

  • Look at where formal jobs are, not only where colleges are. A state can produce many graduates and still add few formal jobs for young people. If the plan is a salaried career, ask early whether it may mean working in another state, and talk about it as a family. Career Compass
  • Check what "a job" means in the offer. In several states more than half of regular non-farm employees have no social security benefit. Before accepting, ask whether the job includes EPF, a written contract and paid leave. Getting your first job
  • Compare pay with the cost of living. Average pay is higher in Maharashtra, Telangana and Karnataka and their large cities, but this data does not include rent or living costs. Weigh both before deciding to move.
  • Plan for daughters' first jobs as seriously as sons'. Women graduates face higher unemployment than men in every state compared. Internships, early work experience and a clear job-search plan matter even more. Guidance Hub
  • Counsellors: use state figures as context, not prediction. They are averages across many courses and colleges, and survey figures for small groups carry wide uncertainty.

Methodology & sources

Method

This report uses public data already collected and checked in the open repository siddiquema/state-jobs-gap, pinned at commit d8703d6. That repository's own validation re-derives its figures from the raw files by a second route; its log has 289 checks, all passing, and is copied into this report's data folder. Every number printed here was then recomputed from the copied CSV files by data/recompute.py, which writes data/validation_log.csv: one row per number, with its source table, data row and recompute result. Graduate unemployment, women's participation and undergraduate pass-outs for each state were also re-read from the original PLFS PDF and AISHE Excel file.

  • Year. AISHE and EPFO figures are for 2023-24, the latest year in which both are available. PLFS figures are for calendar year 2025.
  • Formal entry per 100 young people = EPFO net payroll, ages 18–21 plus 22–25, FY2023-24 ÷ AISHE population aged 18–23, 2023-24 × 100. The EPFO figure is from the newest release (20 Sep 2025), because EPFO revises past months.
  • Graduates per 100 young people = AISHE undergraduate pass-outs, 2023-24 ÷ the same population × 100.
  • Usual status (ps+ss) classifies a person by their main activity over the previous year. PLFS wage figures use the current weekly status (CWS).
  • 95% range of a state's overall unemployment rate = rate × (1 ± 1.96 × published RSE), from PLFS Appendix B, Table (1RSE). This is a calculation from published figures, not a new estimate.
  • Comparison set: West Bengal, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Kerala, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, plus West Bengal's neighbours Odisha, Jharkhand, Bihar, Assam and Sikkim.

Rules this report follows

  • Every number traces to a row in the number-by-number table below, and to a row in data/validation_log.csv.
  • No invented, estimated or modelled statistics. Where data does not exist, we say so.
  • No claims about causes. The data shows where formal jobs and pay differ; it does not show why.
  • Practitioner commentary is labelled as opinion.

Limitations

  • EPFO is formal payroll only. It misses most government jobs, small firms and self-employment. Its age band is 18–25, not "graduates". How EPFO assigns a worker to a state is not stated in its releases and is unconfirmed; it may follow the employer's registration.
  • Net payroll is not jobs created. It is new subscribers, minus exits, plus rejoiners. This applies to the West Bengal figure of 4,57,697 above.
  • AISHE out-turn counts pass-outs, not job-seekers, and counts them where the institution is. Its population figures are projections based on Census 2011.
  • PLFS is a sample survey. State-by-education figures have no published margin of error. Small groups, such as women graduates in smaller states, and Sikkim as a whole, rest on few respondents.
  • Pay is an average (mean), not a median, for all regular employees, not new graduates, and is not adjusted for living costs.
  • No field of study. None of these sources links a course to a job, so this report says nothing about which course leads where.
  • Different years. EPFO and AISHE are 2023-24; PLFS is 2025. The comparison is of patterns, not a year-matched calculation.
