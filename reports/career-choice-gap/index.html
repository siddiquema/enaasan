The Career Choice Gap

Report No. 1 counted the students India's colleges send out each year. This report asks what happens next: how many young graduates who want work cannot find it, and how that compares with young people who stopped studying earlier or took a diploma. It uses the newest official annual jobs survey, released by MoSPI on 27 March 2026.

In India, more education goes with a higher chance of being unemployed when young. A young graduate is more than twice as likely to be unemployed as the average young person, and almost five times as likely as one who stopped after middle school. Young women graduates fare worst. Young people with a diploma or certificate course do markedly better than graduates on both unemployment and access to salaried jobs.

The official survey does not break unemployment down by subject studied, so it cannot say whether Arts or Engineering graduates fare better. What it shows clearly is that a degree on its own is no longer a reliable route to a job.

Latest reading · April–June 2026. The most recent quarterly survey puts urban unemployment for all ages 15+ at 6.7% in April–June 2026 (6.6% the quarter before), and urban women's unemployment at 8.7%. These use the weekly measure, which reads higher than the annual figures in the rest of this report. Source: PLFS Quarterly Bulletin, April–June 2026, released 10 August 2026 [3].

Findings

Finding 1 Data

The more a young person has studied, the more likely they are to be unemployed

Among people aged 15–29, unemployment rises with every step of education, from 2.3% for those who never went to school to 22.3% for graduates and 24.7% for postgraduates.

24.7%
unemployment among young postgraduates, the highest of any education level (never schooled: 2.3%)
Source [1]

This pattern has a known explanation. Young people with little schooling usually cannot afford to stay without work, so they take whatever is available, often casual or family work. Graduates are more likely to wait for a job that matches their qualification. A high graduate unemployment rate therefore reflects both a shortage of suitable jobs and the ability of some families to support a longer search.

Across all ages 15 and above, the gap narrows but remains: graduates 11.2%, postgraduates 10.0%, everyone 3.1% (Table 24 [1]).

Finding 2 Data

Young women graduates face the steepest odds

Women make up the majority of India's graduates in Arts, Science and Commerce (Report No. 1). In the job market they face far higher unemployment than men with the same degree. Among young postgraduates, nearly one woman in three who wants work is unemployed.

28.4%
unemployment among young women graduates, vs 19.4% for men
Source [1]

Finding 3 Data

Diploma holders do better than graduates on jobs and salaried work

Young people whose highest qualification is a diploma or certificate course have lower unemployment than graduates (13.8% vs 22.3%). More of those in the labour force hold regular salaried jobs: 53% against 39% for graduates.

Finding 4 Data

Salaried jobs are scarce, and many lack basic protections

About 78 lakh students complete an undergraduate degree each year (AISHE 2023-24, Report No. 1). The job market they enter looks like this:

58.2%
of salaried employees outside agriculture have no written job contract
PLFS 2025, Statement 10 [1]
  • 23.6% of all workers hold a regular wage or salaried job. Most work is self-employment (56.2%) or casual labour (20.2%). (Statement 7)
  • 43.0% of workers are still in agriculture. In towns and cities, the biggest employers are other services (26.3%), trade, hotels and restaurants (23.8%) and manufacturing (20.3%). (Statement 8)
  • 58.2% of salaried employees outside agriculture have no written job contract, and 51.7% are not eligible for any social security benefit. (Statement 10)
  • ₹22,699 a month is the average salary of a regular employee: ₹24,217 for men and ₹18,353 for women. (Statement 11, weekly measure)

The gap is not only between degrees and jobs. It is between the salaried, protected office job most graduates and their families expect and the work the economy actually offers.

Where students and parents can start

  • Treat a degree as the start, not the plan. A graduate competes with lakhs of others holding the same degree. Decide early what you will add to it: a specific skill, internships, a language, or a portfolio of real work. How to get your first job
  • Take diplomas and certificate courses seriously. In 2025, young people with a diploma or certificate as their highest qualification had lower unemployment and more salaried jobs than graduates. Earning after 10th
  • Ask what the job will look like, not only the salary. More than half of salaried employees outside farming have no written contract. A contract, paid leave and PF are worth checking before you accept an offer. Gig and field work guide
  • Daughters need a plan as much as sons. Young women graduates face the highest unemployment of any group. Families can help by supporting job searches beyond the home town and early work experience during college. Guidance Hub for parents

Methodology & sources

Method

Every labour figure in this report comes from the Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) Annual Report 2025, published by the National Statistics Office, Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI). It covers January–December 2025 and surveyed 2,70,472 households and 11,48,634 people. The "latest reading" box uses the PLFS Quarterly Bulletin for April–June 2026. Graduate counts come from AISHE 2023-24 (see Report No. 1). No figures were estimated or modelled.

  • Unemployment rate is the share of people in the labour force (working or seeking work) who are unemployed. People who are studying or not seeking work are excluded.
  • Usual status (ps+ss) classifies a person by their main activity over the previous 365 days. Unless stated otherwise, every figure here uses this measure.
  • Youth means ages 15–29, as defined by PLFS.
  • Education level is the highest level successfully completed. "Graduate" means a completed bachelor's degree in any subject.
  • Derived figures: labour-force shares in Finding 3 divide each activity's share of the population (Table 21) by the labour-force share for that education level. "More than twice" compares 22.3% with 9.9% (2.3×); "almost five times" compares 22.3% with 4.6% (4.8×).

Limitations

  • No field of study. PLFS records education level, not subject. This report makes no claim about which stream leads to better employment.
  • Not comparable with earlier years. MoSPI redesigned the PLFS sample from January 2025 and states that 2025 estimates may not be strictly comparable with earlier annual reports. This report does not compare 2025 with earlier years.
  • Weekly and annual measures differ. The quarterly figure in the "latest reading" box uses the current weekly status measure, which reads higher than usual status. The two should not be compared directly.
  • Sample sizes vary. Estimates for smaller groups, such as diploma holders or young women postgraduates, rest on fewer survey respondents.
  • Unemployment is not the whole picture. Many employed graduates work in jobs below their qualification, which this survey does not measure.
